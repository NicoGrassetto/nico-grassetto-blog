# What is PTU?

To understand PTU, you first need to understand why you get 429s in the first place. When you deploy a model on Microsoft Foundry, you can choose between two modes: **pay-as-you-go (PAYG)** or **provisioned (PTU)**. PAYG is the default and the most common: your deployment shares a pool of Azure capacity with every other tenant in the region, and Microsoft enforces quota-based rate limits — tokens-per-minute (TPM) and requests-per-minute (RPM) — to keep that shared pool fair. The moment your traffic exceeds those limits, the service starts returning `429 Too Many Requests` until the spike subsides.

This isn't a Microsoft-specific quirk. Every hyperscaler (OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex, Mistral) does some version of the same thing — shared capacity has to be rationed somehow.

**Provisioned Throughput Units (PTU)** are the opposite model: instead of sharing, you reserve. A PTU is a unit of model compute capacity that Azure carves out exclusively for your deployment. With a PTU deployment you're not competing with anyone else for capacity, and you're not subject to PAYG's TPM/RPM throttling — your ceiling is whatever you've provisioned.

I find flow analogies particularly helpful here.

Think of your deployment as a pipe between your client and the model, and each request as a drop of water flowing through it. **The number of PTUs you've provisioned is the pipe's diameter** — it determines how much traffic can flow side-by-side at any given moment. Push more water than the pipe can carry and pressure builds up at the inlet; the overflow comes back as 429s.

So to handle more traffic, you make the pipe wider — i.e. you provision more PTUs. But there's a catch: how wide you're *allowed* to make it is governed by your **quota**. Quota is the maximum pipe diameter Microsoft permits you to install in a given subscription, region, and deployment type. It's a ceiling, not a bill — you don't pay for quota, you only pay for the PTUs you actually deploy against it. If you want a wider pipe than your current quota allows, you file a quota-increase request.

A wider pipe doesn't make the water flow any faster — per-request latency is unchanged — it just means more requests can fit through at the same time before anyone has to wait. Think of the difference between riding a shared bus (PAYG) and owning a car (PTU): the bus may or may not have a seat when you need it and runs on its own schedule; your own car is always there.

Now let's come back to our initial story. The reality of any production app is that you have a **floor of demand** you always need to meet, and **spikes** you have to plan for on top of it. How aggressively you plan for those spikes depends on your sector.

TikTok (for lack of a better example) needs to be on, always — so they almost certainly over-provision, because the business can't afford to go down. The same applies to critical infrastructure: if lives depend on your service, you plan for well beyond any realistic peak. Our airline example sits somewhere in the middle: what was at stake was customer satisfaction and, ultimately, the airline's reputation. How much of the peak you choose to cover is a strategic call, and it belongs with whoever owns the product (the PM, the engineering lead, the business owner — whoever signs off on the cost/risk tradeoff).

It's almost never financially realistic to provision for every conceivable peak. A sensible baseline is to cover the **floor demand** — what you know you'll serve 24/7 — and then cover a chosen percentage of the **recurrent peaks**. In our airline case the peaks come from strikes and travel disruptions; for a commuter-rail operator they'd be the morning and evening rush hours; for TikTok the "peak" is barely a peak at all because the floor is already close to the ceiling.

# PTU strategies
Ultimately, it boils down to two routes: underspending (under-provisioning) and ramping up as you go, or overspending (over-provisioning). The former is more budget-friendly but risks 429s if your traffic spikes beyond your provisioned capacity; the latter is more expensive but gives you a buffer to handle unexpected surges without dropping requests.

# How much PTU should you provision
Now that you hopefuly ahve a decent understand of PTU let'sdivew into the calculations.
### Quota vs. PTU

Before you can deploy a single PTU, you need **quota**. Quota is your total PTU allowance in a given region — set by Microsoft per subscription, per region, and per deployment type. It is a ceiling, not a bill: quota itself is free, and you only pay for the PTUs you actually deploy against it.

### This isn't just a Microsoft thing

Every major hyperscaler offers an equivalent reserved-capacity mechanism — the names differ, but the idea is the same: commit to capacity, escape rate limits. A few even fall back to pay-as-you-go automatically when you exceed your reservation.

| Provider | What they call it | Commitment | Auto-overflow to standard? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Microsoft Foundry** | PTU | Hourly / monthly / annual | No |
| **OpenAI** | Reserved Capacity | Enterprise contract | No |
| **Anthropic** | Priority Tier | Monthly | ✅ Yes |
| **AWS Bedrock** | Reserved Tier / Provisioned Throughput | 1–6 months | ✅ Yes (Reserved Tier) |
| **Google Vertex AI** | Provisioned Throughput | 1 week – 1 year | No |
| **Mistral** | Enterprise APIs (SLA-based) | Negotiated | N/A |

Anthropic and Bedrock's Reserved Tier stand out: their automatic overflow means you're never left hanging if you burst past your reservation.

You can reduce your deployment anytime. You can scale down the PTU count on a provisioned deployment through the Foundry portal or Azure CLI whenever you want — no need to delete and recreate it.
However, your reservation is a separate problem. If you already purchased an Azure Reservation for 50 PTUs, that's a financial commitment and you can't just "resize" it downward freely. Your options are:

Exchange it — Azure allows you to exchange a reservation for a new one of a different size (e.g. swap your 50 PTU reservation for a 13 PTU one). The price difference is refunded or charged pro-rata.
Cancel it — Azure allows early cancellation with a 12% early termination fee, and there's a $50,000/year cap on refunds.
Keep it and right-size later — If you're still early in the commitment period, you might absorb the overage now and plan to right-size at renewal.

If you haven't bought a reservation yet (i.e. you're on hourly billing), then it's simple — just scale the deployment down to 13 PTUs and your hourly charges drop immediately. No penalties.
The practical step right now:

```bash
az cognitiveservices account deployment update \
  --name <myResourceName> \
  --resource-group <myResourceGroupName> \
  --deployment-name <myDeploymentName> \
  --sku-capacity 13
```

What happens if I want to decrease my PTU?


You can increase or decrease the PTU count of an existing provisioned deployment at any time — via the Foundry portal or the API/CLI. No delete-and-recreate needed. Key facts:

Billing adjusts immediately, prorated to the minute, to the new PTU count.
Scaling down releases capacity permanently — the freed PTUs go back to the regional pool, and there's no guarantee you can get that same capacity back if you scale up later.
Reservations are NOT affected by resizing. If a reservation covers the deployment and you scale below the reserved quantity, the reservation keeps billing at its original PTU amount — you just get unused reservation coverage.

The four constraints the doc calls out when scaling **down**:

| Constraint | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Releases capacity permanently** | Freed PTUs go back to the regional pool — no guarantee you can scale back up later |
| **Billing adjusts immediately** | New (lower) PTU count is charged from the moment the resize completes, prorated to the minute |
| **Reservations aren't affected** | If covered by a reservation, scaling the deployment down leaves the reservation at its original quantity → you get **unused reservation coverage** (you keep paying for it) |
| **Scaling up later** | Requires capacity availability — verify before planning |

**Portal:** Foundry portal → your deployment → Edit → lower the PTU count.

**CLI:**

```bash

  --deployment-name <myDeploymentName> \
```

## TODO PTU and reservation

The right idea, but two different *axes* — capacity vs. price. Here's the mental model:

| Step | What it is | Do you pay? |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| **1. Quota** | *Permission.* A ceiling on how many PTUs you're allowed to deploy in a given subscription, region, and deployment type. | Free — it's just a limit |
| **2. PTU deployment** | *Capacity.* You actually create a provisioned deployment that consumes some of that quota. This is the running throughput. | Yes — **hourly** by default |
| **3. Reservation** | *Price.* A financial commitment (1-month or 1-year term) to pre-pay for N PTUs at a big discount vs. the hourly rate. | Yes — but much cheaper per PTU |

The key thing that trips people up: **a reservation is not a technical thing — it deploys nothing and grants no capacity.** It's purely a billing discount that automatically maps onto whatever provisioned deployments you already have.

So the layers are independent:

- **Quota, no deployment** → allowed, but nothing running — pay $0.
- **Deployment, no reservation** → running, paying the expensive hourly rate.
- **Deployment + reservation** → running, paying the cheap committed rate.
- **Reservation, no (or smaller) deployment** → you committed financially but aren't using it = wasted money ("unused reservation coverage" — exactly the scale-down trap above).

**Analogy:**

- Quota = the size of the parking lot the city *lets* you build (permission, free).
- PTU deployment = cars you actually park (metered hourly).
- Reservation = a prepaid monthly parking pass — same spot, much cheaper than feeding the meter, but you pay even on days you don't park.

So the real flow is: **request quota → deploy PTUs (billed hourly) → optionally buy a reservation to discount those PTUs.** You never *need* a reservation to run PTU; it's just the cost-optimization layer on top.

## TODO Scope of PTU quota (notes to reuse)

Short answer: **PTU quota is not just "per subscription".** It's scoped along **three** dimensions simultaneously — subscription × region × deployment type — and it is shared across models, not per-model.

Verbatim from Microsoft Learn:

**On scope** (from [What is provisioned throughput for Foundry Models?](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/provisioned-throughput#what-is-ptu-quota)):

> "PTU quota is the maximum number of PTUs you can deploy **per subscription, per region, and per deployment type**. Quota is a policy limit enforced by Azure, and it has no associated cost. Quota is scoped at the offering level (Global Provisioned, Data Zone Provisioned, and Regional Provisioned are separate quota pools) and at the region level (for example, quota in East US doesn't apply to West Europe)."

**On model independence** (same page, "Provisioned throughput units"):

> "**Model-independent**: The same PTU quota can be used to deploy any supported model. You don't buy PTUs for a specific model."

And from the [billing page](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/provisioned-throughput-billing#how-ptu-billing-works):

> "Your PTU quota is shared across all supported models in a region and deployment type. The same PTU pool can be used to deploy any supported model."

### Implication for the table above

The row **"1. Quota"** previously said *"for a given model + region + deployment type"* — that's **wrong**. Quota is *not* per-model. A single quota pool in `(subscription, region, deployment type)` can be spent on any supported model in that pool. The correct scoping is: `subscription + region + deployment type`.

### Don't confuse PTU quota with Azure Reservations

These are different axes:

| Concept | Scope | Per what? |
|---|---|---|
| **PTU quota** | Per subscription **×** per region **×** per deployment type. Model-independent. | A *permission ceiling*. |
| **Azure Reservation** | Flexible — resource group, subscription, group of subscriptions in a management group, or whole billing account. Per deployment type. Global reservations are not region-bound. | A *financial discount*. |

Verbatim on reservation scope (from the [billing page](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/provisioned-throughput-billing#key-reservation-facts)):

> "**Flexibly scoped** — A reservation can be scoped to cover an individual resource group or subscription, a group of subscriptions in a management group, or all subscriptions in a billing account."
